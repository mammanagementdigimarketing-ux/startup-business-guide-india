# What Happens When a Private Limited Company Stops Operating but Is Not Legally Closed?

When a private limited company stops trading, its legal existence does not automatically come to an end. Unless it completes a recognised closure process and its name is struck off or it is wound up, the company remains registered under the Companies Act, 2013. It may therefore continue to have annual filing, accounting, tax and governance responsibilities even when it has no customers, employees or revenue.

Directors should not treat inactivity as closure: they should either maintain the company’s compliance, apply for dormant status where eligible, or assess whether voluntary strike-off is appropriate.

## Does an Inactive Company Still Have Legal Obligations?

Yes. A company that has not carried on business generally remains responsible for applicable statutory requirements until its legal status changes. The absence of sales or bank transactions does not, by itself, remove filing obligations.

Depending on the company’s circumstances, these can include maintaining books and statutory registers, preparing financial statements, conducting board and shareholder meetings, holding an annual general meeting where required, and filing annual returns and financial statements with the Registrar of Companies (ROC).

Under Sections 92 and 137 of the Companies Act, 2013, companies are generally required to file annual returns and financial statements in the prescribed forms, commonly MGT-7 or MGT-7A and AOC-4 or the applicable variant. Exemptions or modified requirements may apply to particular company categories, but there is no blanket exemption simply because a private limited company has stopped operating.

## What Can Happen If the Company Is Left Inactive?

### 1. Annual Filing Defaults and Additional Costs

If the company does not file its required financial statements or annual returns, it can accumulate statutory penalties and additional filing fees. The amount depends on the form, period of delay, applicable provisions and any amendments or relief schemes in force.

Continued non-compliance can make a later closure application more difficult because overdue filings may need to be completed before the company can apply for strike-off.

### 2. Possible Consequences for Directors

Director responsibilities do not disappear when business activity stops. In particular, Section 164(2) provides for disqualification in specified circumstances where a company has not filed financial statements or annual returns for a continuous period of three financial years.

The provision can affect a director’s eligibility for appointment or reappointment in other companies for the statutory period. Its application depends on the facts and statutory exceptions, so directors should not assume that an inactive company is harmless to their future roles.

### 3. Tax and Other Statutory Obligations May Continue

A company may still need to review income-tax return filing, tax audit applicability, TDS statements and payments, GST returns or cancellation, employee-related obligations, licences and contractual liabilities.

The exact requirements depend on its registrations, transactions, status and applicable tax law. ROC filings and tax filings are separate systems: completing one does not automatically satisfy the other.

### 4. Outstanding Liabilities Remain Relevant

Loans, unpaid vendor invoices, employee dues, statutory dues, litigation and guarantees do not vanish because operations have stopped.

Directors and shareholders should identify and address obligations before choosing a closure route. A company should not use strike-off to avoid legitimate creditor claims or conceal unresolved disputes.

## Dormant Company Status: An Option for Businesses on Hold

If the company is not currently operating but may be used again, it may consider applying for dormant-company status under Section 455 of the Companies Act, 2013.

A company formed for a future project or to hold an asset or intellectual property, and one that has had no significant accounting transaction, may qualify if it meets the statutory conditions.

Dormant status is not the same as closure. The company remains incorporated and must comply with the requirements that continue to apply to dormant companies, including the prescribed annual return in Form MSC-3 and other applicable conditions.

It is therefore more suitable for a company that intends to preserve its legal identity for future use rather than permanently discontinue it.

Before applying, check eligibility, outstanding filings, liabilities and the latest MCA form requirements. A company should not assume that it automatically becomes dormant after a period without transactions; it must obtain the status through the prescribed process.

## Voluntary Strike-Off: When the Company Is No Longer Needed

Where the business has permanently ceased and the company is no longer required, voluntary strike-off under Section 248(2) may be an option.

Broadly, the company must have extinguished its liabilities and pass a special resolution or obtain consent from members holding at least 75% of its paid-up share capital. The statutory grounds and restrictions must also be satisfied.

Section 248(1) includes grounds such as failure to commence business within one year of incorporation, or not carrying on business or operations for the immediately preceding two financial years and not having applied for dormant status during that period.

The company must also check restrictions under the Act and the Companies (Removal of Names of Companies from the Register of Companies) Rules, 2016. Certain companies, including those with specified pending proceedings, charges or regulatory circumstances, may not be eligible for the simplified route.

### What Is Involved in the STK-2 Process?

An eligible company applies to the Registrar, Centre for Processing Accelerated Corporate Exit (C-PACE), using Form STK-2.

The application generally includes:

- A notarised indemnity bond in Form STK-3 from each director.
- An affidavit in Form STK-4 from each director.
- A statement of accounts certified by a practising Chartered Accountant and made up to a date not more than 30 days before the application.
- The relevant shareholder approval.
- Details of pending litigation, if any.

Additional documents or regulatory no-objection certificates may be required in particular cases.

Before filing, the company must review overdue annual filings. The Rules require overdue financial statements and annual returns to be filed up to the end of the financial year in which business operations ceased, subject to the applicable provisions.

The STK-2 filing fee is ₹10,000 under the amended Rules. The ROC reviews the application and statutory public notice process; filing the form does not itself dissolve the company. The company is treated as dissolved only after the prescribed final action and notice.

## A Practical Decision Checklist for Directors

- Confirm whether the company has genuinely ceased operations or is only temporarily inactive.
- Review MCA master data, annual filing status, statutory registers and pending ROC notices.
- Prepare financial statements and identify overdue AOC-4 and MGT-7/MGT-7A filings.
- Check income-tax, GST, TDS, employee, licence and contractual obligations separately.
- List all assets, liabilities, loans, charges, creditor claims and pending litigation.
- Consider dormant status if the company may be used again and the statutory conditions are met.
- Consider voluntary strike-off only if the company is eligible and liabilities can be extinguished.
- Keep copies of resolutions, filed forms, acknowledgements and final ROC orders.

## Taking the Right Next Step

Leaving a private limited company inactive without a compliance plan can create avoidable filing defaults, costs and director-related consequences. Owners should decide whether to maintain the company, obtain dormant status or pursue a lawful closure.

The best route depends on the company’s filing history, assets, liabilities, regulatory position and future plans.
